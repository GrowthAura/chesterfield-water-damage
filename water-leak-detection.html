How do you find a hidden leak in a Chesterfield home when the water bill jumps but nothing looks wet?

Start with the meter. Turn off every fixture and watch the leak indicator on the water meter. If it moves, water is escaping somewhere, and moisture meters and thermal imaging narrow down the location.

A Missouri American Water bill that climbs without a change in habits is often the first sign. A running toilet is the most common cause and the easiest to rule out: put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and see whether color reaches the bowl without flushing.

The meter test, step by step

Make sure no water is being used: no taps, ice maker, washer, dishwasher or irrigation.

Find the meter, usually in the basement where the line enters, or in a pit near the street.

Look at the small leak indicator dial or the digital flow reading.

If it shows flow, close the main valve inside the house and look again.

If flow stops, the leak is inside the house. If it continues, it is in the service line between the meter and the house.

Signs of a hidden leak

A musty smell in one room or closet. Paint or wallpaper bubbling. Baseboards separating or swelling. A warm spot on the floor. Hardwood cupping in one area. Stains on a ceiling. The sound of running water when everything is off. Mold appearing on a wall with no obvious cause.

How we locate it

Thermal imaging

An infrared camera shows temperature differences on surfaces. Wet materials are cooler as water evaporates, and hot-water leaks show as warm trails.

Moisture meters

Pinless meters scan walls and floors without marking them. Pin meters confirm how wet the material is and how deep.

Mapping the pattern

Water travels along framing and pipes. By following the readings from the wettest point outward, we work back to where it starts.

Small inspection openings

Where needed, a small hole lets us look inside a cavity before committing to a larger opening.

Where leaks hide in local homes

Shower valves and drains in second-floor bathrooms.

Supply lines in walls behind kitchens and laundry rooms.

Refrigerator and dishwasher connections.

Pipes in the floor of rooms over garages.

Hose bib lines that split inside the wall after a freeze.

Basement bathroom plumbing under the slab.

Irrigation lines and backflow devices outside.

What we do and what a plumber does

Our focus is moisture: finding the wet area, tracing it to the likely source, and drying and repairing the building afterward. A plumber repairs the pipe. For leaks under a concrete slab or in a buried service line, plumbers and leak specialists use acoustic and pressure testing equipment, and we will tell you when the job calls for that.

After the leak is found

The wet materials still need drying, and long-running leaks often mean mold. See Structural Drying and Mold Remediation. If the leak turns out to be a failed line that has already flooded a room, see Burst Pipe Water Damage.

Cost and insurance

An inspection with thermal imaging and moisture readings is typically in the low hundreds of dollars, depending on the size of the home and how long the search takes. Insurance may cover the resulting water damage if the leak was sudden and hidden, but generally not the repair of the pipe itself, and long-term leaks are often excluded.
